File 1 Chapter 1: Magna Carta and the Will of Power

  • Fact Verified: Magna Carta (1215) established the foundational bedrock of the Rule of Law, forcing the historic principle that justice must be based on a neutral framework rather than the personal, arbitrary "will" of the entity holding administrative power.
  • The Trap Realized: When the modern state strips its own inspectors of liability while forcing builders to pay into the system, it fundamentally breaks this ancient constitutional promise.

File 2 Chapter 1b: The Victorian Explosion & Building Bye-Laws

  • Fact Verified: The rapid urban boom of the Industrial Revolution caused catastrophic sanitation crises, forcing the state to abandon its completely "hands-off" approach to speculative housing construction.
  • The Milestone Act: The Public Health Act 1875 systematically consolidated local powers and introduced the 1877 "Model Bye-laws," creating the country's first written checklists for structural walls and mandatory sewer links.
Primary Fact Verification Link: UK National Archives: Public Health Act 1875 Portal →

File 3 Chapter 2: The Public Health Act 1936

  • Fact Verified: Section 64 established an objective blueprint checklist to handle the massive fragmentation of local building regulations across the country.
  • The Dynamic: The structural blueprint approval process was entirely "in personam blind"—meaning it was completely indifferent to the identity, background, or commercial intent of the person behind the build. It was constructed solely for basic public safety metrics, offering zero financial insulation for the developer.

File 4 Chapter 3: The 1970s Pivot — The DPA and Anns

  • Fact Verified: Section 1 of the Defective Premises Act 1972 locked strict civil liability onto the builder to ensure newly provided dwellings are "fit for human habitation."
  • The Illusory Backstop: The ruling in Anns v Merton LBC (1978) briefly created a common law duty where local councils could be sued for the financial repair costs if an inspector negligently signed off on an "imminent danger to health." This created an illusion of state accountability that was later crushed.

File 5 Chapter 4: November 1979 Deregulation

  • Fact Verified: Following the May 1979 general election, the Thatcher administration launched a concerted administrative strategy to aggressively downsize public spending and transition risk to the market.
  • The Policy Pivot: In late 1979, Environment Secretary Michael Heseltine delivered a pivotal policy mandate to Parliament and the NHBC, confirming the government’s explicit intention to drastically thin down local authority parameters, slash the building codes, and privatise inspectors.
Primary Fact Verification Link: University of London Press: Recasting Building Control History Records →

File 6 Chapter 5: The Building Act 1984

  • Fact Verified: The Building Act 1984 completely upended the state monopoly by introducing privatised "Approved Inspectors" (with the NHBC acting as the primary pioneer), allowing commercial entities to compete with local councils to issue building control passes.
  • The Rule Slashing: The subsequent 1985 building regulations condensed the historic, exhaustive 306-page manual of prescriptive design rules into a minimal 24-page framework of open "functional requirements," compromising safety oversight.
Primary Fact Verification Link: UK Legislation Services: Building Act 1984 Regulatory Framework →

File 7 Chapter 6: The Climax — Murphy and the Liability Trap

  • The Trap Sealed: The Court of Appeal case of Herons Court (2019) extended this absolute financial immunity to all private inspectors (explicitly targeting the NHBC), establishing that whether a builder relies on a state or private inspector, both pathways carry zero accountability, leaving 100% of the financial liability permanently locked onto the builder.
  • Fact Verified: The landmark common law precedent of Murphy v Brentwood (1991) completely immunised local councils from negligence claims for economic repair costs, declaring that discovering a structural defect before an accident is merely an unprotected financial loss.
