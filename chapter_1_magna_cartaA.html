Chapter 1: The Foundations of Constitutional Law and State Accountability

Timeline: 1215 (Magna Carta) to the early 19th Century

The Origin of the State's Authority

To understand how a system could eventually trap an individual citizen or business owner, we must go back to the very foundation of English law: Magna Carta (1215). Magna Carta established that the King (the state) was not above the law. It introduced the concept of due process and basic statutory fairness.

The Ancient Rule of Sovereign Immunity

Despite establishing that the King must follow the law, English common law simultaneously maintained a contradictory doctrine: "The King can do no wrong" (Sovereign Immunity). This rule meant that the state could pass laws, mandate taxes, and govern your daily actions, but if the state’s agents harmed you or caused you economic loss, you could not sue the Crown in a private civil court for damages.

How This Laid the Historical Groundwork

For centuries, the law separated public governance from private compensation. The state existed to maintain public order, collect revenues, and regulate commerce, but it never acted as an insurer for private investments or commercial risks. This deep constitutional boundary meant that when administrative regulatory bodies were later created in the 19th and 20th centuries, they naturally inherited this historical shield of non-accountability.
